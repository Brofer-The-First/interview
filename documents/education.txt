During my military service I learned Arabic and discovered a genuine interest in languages. This led me to look for academic programs involving linguistics, and I was drawn to the "Biology & Linguistics with emphasis on Neuroscience" program at Tel Aviv University. What attracted me was the interdisciplinary approach and the Sagol School of Neuroscience.

In biology, I enjoy understanding how nature works at all its levels — physics, chemistry, biology, genetics, neurobiology, neuroanatomy — and how different scientific disciplines evolved. In linguistics, I enjoy theoretical linguistics and the underlying concepts behind human language, as well as the research methodology, especially in psycholinguistics.

As part of the BSc, I worked on a research project in Prof. Einat Shetreet's lab, which focuses on pragmatic language processing. My project explored the naturalness of double negatives ("not not good") across different adjectives, as a function of whether they are more scalar or binary (e.g., "not not good" vs. "not not dead"). The project was presented as a poster at XPRAG.it 2020(21), a prominent Experimental Pragmatics conference.

The MSc in Neuroscience at Sagol was a natural progression from the BSc. My thesis explored whether misleading morphological cues for grammatical gender of nouns cause prolonged processing. In simpler terms: in Hebrew, the word "shulchanot" (tables) has a feminine-sounding suffix (-ot), even though "shulchan" is masculine. I found that processing of such nouns is indeed prolonged, and more interestingly, this effect is independent of the experimental task — even when subjects are not explicitly asked about gender, they still take longer to react to these nouns.

Working with Prof. Meltzer-Asscher and Prof. Shetreet was an excellent experience. They provided all the support I needed while encouraging me to work independently and show initiative.

While I thoroughly enjoyed broadening my horizons during my studies, I realized that a career in academia is not what I desire. I enjoy learning new things, but the day-to-day reality of academic work — recruiting test subjects, writing articles, presenting research at conferences, networking with fellow academics — is not what drives me.
